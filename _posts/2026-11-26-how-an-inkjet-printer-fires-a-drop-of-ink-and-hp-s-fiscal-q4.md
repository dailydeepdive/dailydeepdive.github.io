### One day ago: How doctors calm a quivering heart with electric pulses (Medtronic)

*Deep dive covering Tuesday, Nov. 24, 2026, in the Wednesday, Nov. 25 briefing*<sup class="cite"><a href="#ref-53" title="Source 53">53</a></sup>.

**What was covered.** In 1998 Bordeaux cardiologist Michel Haïssaguerre reported that in 41 of 45 patients with atrial fibrillation, the stray signals started inside the four veins that bring blood from the lungs<sup class="cite"><a href="#ref-53" title="Source 53">53</a></sup>. The deep dive explained how doctors now wall those veins off, and how a new way of doing it flipped a market in under three years<sup class="cite"><a href="#ref-53" title="Source 53">53</a></sup>.

**How it works.** In atrial fibrillation, the heart's upper chambers quiver at roughly 300 to 600 signals a minute instead of beating in rhythm, like an orchestra drowned out by hecklers in the doorways; blood pools, can clot, and stroke risk rises about fivefold<sup class="cite"><a href="#ref-53" title="Source 53">53</a></sup>.

1. **The fix.** A doctor threads a thin tube (a catheter) from the groin into the heart and scars a ring of tissue around each lung vein, a firebreak the stray signals can't cross; this is called ablation<sup class="cite"><a href="#ref-53" title="Source 53">53</a></sup>.
2. **The old way: heat or cold.** For 25 years the scar was made by heating tissue above about 50°C or freezing it, which occasionally injured the esophagus next door; a hole between heart and esophagus occurred in about 0.025% of cases but killed 66% of those patients<sup class="cite"><a href="#ref-53" title="Source 53">53</a></sup>.
3. **The new way: electric pulses.** Pulsed field ablation fires microsecond bursts that punch holes in cell walls. Heart muscle cells die at a weaker field (0.65 kV/cm in an FDA lab study) than esophagus cells (1.67 kV/cm), so doctors can kill one and spare the other<sup class="cite"><a href="#ref-53" title="Source 53">53</a></sup>.

**Why it matters.** Pulsed field ablation went from zero to about 78% of U.S. ablation catheter spending by early 2026<sup class="cite"><a href="#ref-53" title="Source 53">53</a></sup>. Medtronic overtook first-mover Boston Scientific thanks to Affera, a $925 million startup purchase that combined the catheter with a live 3D map of the heart; its ablation sales grew 58% last quarter<sup class="cite"><a href="#ref-53" title="Source 53">53</a></sup>.

**Test yourself**

1. *Why do the misfires start in the lung veins?* Muscle cells that extend into the pulmonary veins can fire on their own; Haïssaguerre found that is where the trigger sat in 41 of 45 patients, so walling off the veins stops the chaos at its source<sup class="cite"><a href="#ref-53" title="Source 53">53</a></sup>.
2. *What makes pulsed field ablation safer for the esophagus?* Heart muscle dies at a much weaker electric field than esophagus tissue, about 0.65 versus 1.67 kV/cm, so the field can be tuned to kill one and spare the other, while heat spreads to whatever is nearby<sup class="cite"><a href="#ref-53" title="Source 53">53</a></sup>.
3. *Why does owning the mapping system help Medtronic sell more catheters?* Affera's catheter is tied into Affera's own 3D mapping system, so every hospital that installs the map becomes a repeat buyer of Medtronic catheters<sup class="cite"><a href="#ref-53" title="Source 53">53</a></sup>.

### Three days ago: How your tax return travels through the IRS's 1960s computer (Intuit)

*Deep dive covering Sunday, Nov. 22, 2026, in the Monday, Nov. 23 briefing*<sup class="cite"><a href="#ref-52" title="Source 52">52</a></sup>.

**What was covered.** In 2024, IRS Commissioner Danny Werfel described a software project as "preventing a potential collapse" of the system under the tax code: the Individual Master File, the IRS's master record of every taxpayer's account, designed in the late 1960s<sup class="cite"><a href="#ref-52" title="Source 52">52</a></sup>. The deep dive followed one e-filed return from TurboTax to refund<sup class="cite"><a href="#ref-52" title="Source 52">52</a></sup>.

**How it works.** Picture a bank whose front desk checks your deposit slip in minutes but posts every slip to its giant ledger only once a week<sup class="cite"><a href="#ref-52" title="Source 52">52</a></sup>.

1. **Front door.** Intuit, a licensed transmitter, sends your return to the IRS's Modernized e-File gateway, which checks it against business rules (does the Social Security number match the name, was the dependent already claimed) and accepts or rejects it within 24 hours<sup class="cite"><a href="#ref-52" title="Source 52">52</a></sup>.
2. **Fraud screens.** Hundreds of identity-theft filters run before money leaves; by law, refunds with certain family tax credits wait until mid-February<sup class="cite"><a href="#ref-52" title="Source 52">52</a></sup>.
3. **The old ledger.** Posting to your account ran on assembly and COBOL code encoding decades of tax law<sup class="cite"><a href="#ref-52" title="Source 52">52</a></sup>.
4. **The fix.** After failed rewrites starting in 1999, engineers translated the old code into Java piece by piece and ran old and new side by side until they matched 99% of the time<sup class="cite"><a href="#ref-52" title="Source 52">52</a></sup>.

**Why it matters.** In 2026, the first season on the new engine and after deep staff cuts, the IRS had processed only 92.3% of returns received by early February, versus 99.7% a year earlier, before catching up by May<sup class="cite"><a href="#ref-52" title="Source 52">52</a></sup>. Paper returns, which people key in by hand, are where the system breaks<sup class="cite"><a href="#ref-52" title="Source 52">52</a></sup>.

**Test yourself**

1. *Why doesn't "accepted" mean your return has posted?* Acceptance only means the front-door gateway found no rule violations; the return still has to pass fraud screens and be posted to your account in the master file's batch runs<sup class="cite"><a href="#ref-52" title="Source 52">52</a></sup>.
2. *Why did translating code succeed where rewrites failed?* Rewriting from scratch meant rediscovering decades of tax rules buried in old code; translating it line by line kept that logic intact, and running old and new side by side proved the outputs matched<sup class="cite"><a href="#ref-52" title="Source 52">52</a></sup>.
3. *Why are paper returns the weak point?* They need people to key them in by hand, so staff cuts create backlogs, while e-filed returns flow mostly by computer<sup class="cite"><a href="#ref-52" title="Source 52">52</a></sup>.

## Deep dive: How an inkjet printer fires a drop of ink, and HP's fiscal Q4
{: #deep-dive .deep-dive-head}

In the mid-1970s at Canon in Japan, a hot soldering iron accidentally touched the needle of a syringe full of ink, and ink spurted out<sup class="cite"><a href="#ref-22" title="Source 22">22</a></sup>. Ichiro Endo saw what had happened, and within days his team had a working model of what became the Canon Bubble Jet printer<sup class="cite"><a href="#ref-15" title="Source 15">15</a></sup>. Canon filed its basic patent application in October 1977<sup class="cite"><a href="#ref-23" title="Source 23">23</a></sup>. Around the same time, and independently, John Vaught at HP Laboratories arrived at the same idea<sup class="cite"><a href="#ref-15" title="Source 15">15</a>,<a href="#ref-16" title="Source 16">16</a></sup>.

### The short version

A thermal inkjet printer boils ink on purpose: a tiny heater under each nozzle is pulsed for a few millionths of a second, a vapor bubble forms, and the bubble pushes a drop of ink out of the nozzle<sup class="cite"><a href="#ref-17" title="Source 17">17</a></sup>. HP turned that process into the first mass-marketed personal inkjet printer in 1984<sup class="cite"><a href="#ref-18" title="Source 18">18</a></sup>. HP's business model rests on the cartridges: as then-CEO Enrique Lores said at Davos in 2024, "We lose money on the hardware, we make money on the supplies"<sup class="cite"><a href="#ref-21" title="Source 21">21</a></sup>. Over the last five reported quarters, HP's Supplies revenue changed by between −4% and +1% year over year<sup class="cite"><a href="#ref-7" title="Source 7">7</a>,<a href="#ref-8" title="Source 8">8</a>,<a href="#ref-9" title="Source 9">9</a>,<a href="#ref-10" title="Source 10">10</a>,<a href="#ref-11" title="Source 11">11</a></sup>.

### Key terms

<div class="table-wrap" markdown="1">

| Term | Plain meaning |
| --- | --- |
| Thermal inkjet | Printing by flash-boiling a thin layer of ink so a vapor bubble pushes a drop out of a nozzle (Canon calls it Bubble Jet) |
| Printhead | The chip with the heaters, the tiny ink chambers and the row of nozzles; in HP's ThinkJet it was built into the cartridge and thrown away with it |
| Picoliter | A trillionth of a liter; Canon describes modern inkjet drops as "mere picoliters"<sup class="cite"><a href="#ref-25" title="Source 25">25</a></sup> |
| Piezoelectric inkjet | The main rival method: a piezoelectric element, which flexes when a voltage is applied, pushes the ink out instead of boiling it<sup class="cite"><a href="#ref-27" title="Source 27">27</a></sup> |
| Kogation | The crust of burned ink residue that builds up on a heater and can eventually insulate it from the ink |
| Supplies | HP's name for its ink and toner cartridges; HP reports their revenue but not their profit separately |

</div>

### How it works

**The analogy: a kettle with a hair trigger.** Picture a kettle so small and so fast that it boils, spits and goes quiet in less time than a camera flash, and does it over and over. Each nozzle on an inkjet printhead sits on top of one of these: a tiny chamber of ink with a microscopic heater at the bottom. Heat a thin layer of ink fast enough and it reaches about 250°C before it boils, far above water's usual 100°C, which is what makes the trick work<sup class="cite"><a href="#ref-17" title="Source 17">17</a></sup>.

**How HP found its way there.** At HP Labs, Vaught proposed using a thin-film resistor, a microscopic heating strip of the kind HP already made for its chips, to boil ink into bubbles<sup class="cite"><a href="#ref-16" title="Source 16">16</a></sup>. The first prototype was crude: a single hole punched by hand with a sewing machine needle borrowed from an engineer's wife. It shot fountain pen ink several inches through the air<sup class="cite"><a href="#ref-16" title="Source 16">16</a></sup>. As the HP Journal later put it, "Unknown to HP at this time, Canon, Inc. was independently developing this technology"<sup class="cite"><a href="#ref-16" title="Source 16">16</a></sup>. The two companies later cross-licensed many patents<sup class="cite"><a href="#ref-24" title="Source 24">24</a></sup>. Endo and Vaught later shared awards, including the Optical Society's Edwin H. Land Medal, for their independent inventions<sup class="cite"><a href="#ref-15" title="Source 15">15</a></sup>.

**Mechanism one: the 50-microsecond cycle.** HP engineer Christopher Beatty described one firing in 1996<sup class="cite"><a href="#ref-17" title="Source 17">17</a></sup>. An electrical pulse only a few millionths of a second long superheats a thin layer of ink to about 250°C<sup class="cite"><a href="#ref-17" title="Source 17">17</a></sup>. That layer boils almost all at once across the heater's surface, forming a single bubble rather than the scattered bubbles of a pot on the stove<sup class="cite"><a href="#ref-17" title="Source 17">17</a></sup>. At 5 microseconds the pulse is over; at 15 microseconds the bubble is fully grown and the drop is leaving the nozzle; at 24 microseconds the bubble collapses back onto the heater<sup class="cite"><a href="#ref-17" title="Source 17">17</a></sup>. Refilling the chamber takes another 25 to 50 microseconds; as the bubble collapses, surface tension at the nozzle pulls fresh ink in from the reservoir<sup class="cite"><a href="#ref-17" title="Source 17">17</a>,<a href="#ref-16" title="Source 16">16</a></sup>. From those timings alone, one nozzle could fire roughly 13,000 to 20,000 times a second (my own arithmetic).

**Mechanism two: shrinking the drop.** HP's 1984 ThinkJet had 12 nozzles spaced a quarter of a millimeter apart and printed at 96 dots per inch<sup class="cite"><a href="#ref-16" title="Source 16">16</a>,<a href="#ref-17" title="Source 17">17</a></sup>. Each drop weighed about 3 ten-millionths of a gram<sup class="cite"><a href="#ref-16" title="Source 16">16</a></sup>, about 300 picoliters (my own arithmetic). Canon describes today's drops as "mere picoliters"<sup class="cite"><a href="#ref-25" title="Source 25">25</a></sup>, a small fraction of the ThinkJet's. Smaller drops mean finer dots, which is how inkjets went from blocky letters to photographs.

**Mechanism three: wearing out on purpose.** Boiling ink millions of times is hard on the heater. When the bubble collapses it sends a pressure pulse into the heater's thin films<sup class="cite"><a href="#ref-17" title="Source 17">17</a></sup>, and burned residue, kogation, builds up and can eventually insulate the heater from the ink<sup class="cite"><a href="#ref-16" title="Source 16">16</a></sup>. HP's engineers built the whole printhead into the cartridge and made it disposable, which kept costs down and avoided hoses, plumbing and valves<sup class="cite"><a href="#ref-16" title="Source 16">16</a></sup>. The ThinkJet's head needed to survive about 2 million firings to print its rated 500 pages, and HP predicted 25 million<sup class="cite"><a href="#ref-16" title="Source 16">16</a></sup>. A later protective layer of tantalum improved head life by more than ten times<sup class="cite"><a href="#ref-17" title="Source 17">17</a></sup>. Our read: a disposable head also means every new cartridge brings fresh heaters.

**The rival: squeezing instead of boiling.** Piezoelectric printheads take the other road: a voltage waveform makes a piezoelectric membrane flex, which sends a pressure pulse through the ink with no boiling involved<sup class="cite"><a href="#ref-27" title="Source 27">27</a></sup>. A 2021 review in IEEE Access describes piezo printing as the main focus of the field because of its reliability and its ability to handle a diverse range of inks<sup class="cite"><a href="#ref-27" title="Source 27">27</a></sup>. We did not verify claims about which design is cheaper to make, so they are left out.

### Step by step: printing one letter on a 1984 ThinkJet

1. **The computer sends the letter.** The printer's electronics break it into a grid of dots 12 rows tall, one row per nozzle<sup class="cite"><a href="#ref-16" title="Source 16">16</a></sup>.
2. **The head moves.** The cartridge slides across the page; at each column, the electronics decide which of the 12 heaters to fire<sup class="cite"><a href="#ref-16" title="Source 16">16</a></sup>.
3. **Fire.** A pulse of about 6 microseconds, which HP chose as the best compromise between saving power and tolerating voltage swings, heats each chosen resistor past ink's boiling point<sup class="cite"><a href="#ref-16" title="Source 16">16</a></sup>.
4. **Bubble and drop.** The vapor bubble pushes a drop out of the nozzle toward the paper; the bubble collapses back onto the heater within microseconds<sup class="cite"><a href="#ref-16" title="Source 16">16</a>,<a href="#ref-17" title="Source 17">17</a></sup>.
5. **Refill and repeat.** Fresh ink flows in and the next column fires; the printer managed about 150 characters per second<sup class="cite"><a href="#ref-16" title="Source 16">16</a></sup>, versus the 120 characters per second that dot-matrix printers typically managed, per TechRadar<sup class="cite"><a href="#ref-19" title="Source 19">19</a></sup>.
6. **Throw it away.** After about 500 pages, the cartridge, ink and printhead were replaced together<sup class="cite"><a href="#ref-16" title="Source 16">16</a></sup>; TechRadar reports cartridges cost $8 each<sup class="cite"><a href="#ref-19" title="Source 19">19</a></sup>.

### Where things stand

**The model, in HP's words.** Lores at Davos in 2024: "We lose money on the hardware, we make money on the supplies"<sup class="cite"><a href="#ref-21" title="Source 21">21</a></sup>. He also said a customer who "doesn't print enough or doesn't use our supplies" is "a bad investment"<sup class="cite"><a href="#ref-20" title="Source 20">20</a></sup>. HP uses chips in its cartridges and firmware it calls Dynamic Security, which can reject cartridges that lack an HP security chip; HP says it discloses this<sup class="cite"><a href="#ref-20" title="Source 20">20</a></sup>. Lores argued that third-party cartridges can carry security risks<sup class="cite"><a href="#ref-21" title="Source 21">21</a></sup>; The Register says it is unaware of cartridge-chip malware outside a lab<sup class="cite"><a href="#ref-21" title="Source 21">21</a></sup>.

**In court.** HP settled a toner lockout class action in 2025 without paying, promising to keep disclosing Dynamic Security and to let users decline firmware updates that include it<sup class="cite"><a href="#ref-21" title="Source 21">21</a></sup>. In an Illinois inkjet case, a judge granted HP's motion to dismiss on Sept. 30, 2025, with leave to amend, and the plaintiffs filed an amended complaint in November<sup class="cite"><a href="#ref-20" title="Source 20">20</a></sup>.

### Why it matters

- **For technology:** inkjet heads are also used to print electronic materials, including complex integrated circuits, and thermal inkjet printers have been adapted to print living cells for tissue-engineering research<sup class="cite"><a href="#ref-26" title="Source 26">26</a></sup>.
- **For consumers:** cheap printers and expensive cartridges put much of the cost of printing in the cartridge, and disputes over chips and firmware decide whether cheaper third-party ink is an option<sup class="cite"><a href="#ref-20" title="Source 20">20</a>,<a href="#ref-21" title="Source 21">21</a></sup>.
- **For HP:** the Printing segment, hardware and supplies together, earned about 61% of the operating profit from HP's two main segments in fiscal 2026 (my own arithmetic)<sup class="cite"><a href="#ref-7" title="Source 7">7</a></sup>; HP does not report Supplies profit separately.

### Talking points

1. An inkjet printer boils ink on purpose: a heater superheats a thin layer of ink to about 250°C in a few microseconds, and the vapor bubble pushes out a drop<sup class="cite"><a href="#ref-17" title="Source 17">17</a></sup>.
2. The whole cycle, from pulse to refill, takes roughly 50 to 75 microseconds (my own arithmetic from Beatty's timings), so each nozzle can fire thousands of times a second<sup class="cite"><a href="#ref-17" title="Source 17">17</a></sup>.
3. Canon's Ichiro Endo and HP's John Vaught invented it independently and at about the same time; Canon filed its basic patent in October 1977, and the two companies later cross-licensed many patents<sup class="cite"><a href="#ref-15" title="Source 15">15</a>,<a href="#ref-23" title="Source 23">23</a>,<a href="#ref-24" title="Source 24">24</a></sup>.
4. HP built the printhead into the throwaway cartridge, so a head only had to last one cartridge, about 2 million firings<sup class="cite"><a href="#ref-16" title="Source 16">16</a></sup>.
5. Piezoelectric heads, the main alternative, squeeze ink with a voltage-driven membrane instead of boiling it<sup class="cite"><a href="#ref-27" title="Source 27">27</a></sup>.
6. Explain it to a friend: HP's stock rose 11.5% after a quarter in which PCs drove revenue growth, while Printing, where fiscal 2026 revenue fell 1%, still earned most of the profit from its two main segments<sup class="cite"><a href="#ref-2" title="Source 2">2</a>,<a href="#ref-7" title="Source 7">7</a></sup>.
