- **Science: a famous "first reptile" fossil turns out to be a fish-like swimmer.** "Lizzie," a 345-million-year-old fossil found in Scotland in 1984 and long treated as one of the earliest land-living ancestors of reptiles, has internal bony gills, a fish-like mouth and thousands of tiny teeth, according to X-ray scans at the European Synchrotron in Grenoble published in Nature<sup class="cite"><a href="#ref-14" title="Source 14">14</a></sup>. The team, led from the American Museum of Natural History, says it lived in water or on the water's edge<sup class="cite"><a href="#ref-14" title="Source 14">14</a></sup>. Why it matters: claws and five-fingered limbs apparently evolved in water-dwelling animals before life moved fully onto land, so some ancient "reptile" footprints may have been made by amphibious animals<sup class="cite"><a href="#ref-14" title="Source 14">14</a></sup>. Caveat: the case rests on one crushed specimen<sup class="cite"><a href="#ref-14" title="Source 14">14</a></sup>.
- **Space: a galaxy-mapping telescope finds a comet.** Images from ESA's Euclid telescope taken Sept. 16-24 caught a new comet about 1.3 billion km from Earth, roughly between Jupiter and Saturn, spotted by University of Helsinki PhD student Mikko Pöntinen with detection software he wrote; it was confirmed on Oct. 8 as C/2026 S1 (Euclid)<sup class="cite"><a href="#ref-15" title="Source 15">15</a></sup>. Why it matters: Euclid was built to map billions of distant galaxies, so it shows that sky surveys can double as comet hunters, and gives ESA practice for coordinating with its future Comet Interceptor mission<sup class="cite"><a href="#ref-15" title="Source 15">15</a></sup>. Caveat: its orbit, which takes thousands of years, will only be pinned down with more observations<sup class="cite"><a href="#ref-15" title="Source 15">15</a></sup>.
- **Physics: one particle of antimatter, held for months.** A Northwestern team led by Gerald Gabrielse trapped a single positron, the antimatter twin of the electron, cooled it to about 0.3 degrees above absolute zero, and kept it in a near-perfect vacuum for months, reporting in Physical Review Letters<sup class="cite"><a href="#ref-16" title="Source 16">16</a></sup>. They also used microwaves to flip it between quantum energy levels<sup class="cite"><a href="#ref-16" title="Source 16">16</a></sup>. Why it matters: the same group measured the electron's magnetism to about one part in 10 trillion, so a matching positron measurement would be the sharpest matter-versus-antimatter comparison ever, and any difference could help explain why the universe is made of matter at all<sup class="cite"><a href="#ref-16" title="Source 16">16</a></sup>. Caveat: this is a demonstration of control; the comparison itself has not been made, and current theory predicts no difference<sup class="cite"><a href="#ref-16" title="Source 16">16</a></sup>.

### One day ago: How doctors calm a quivering heart with electric pulses (Medtronic)

*Deep dive covering Tuesday, Nov. 24, 2026, in the Wednesday, Nov. 25 briefing*<sup class="cite"><a href="#ref-18" title="Source 18">18</a></sup>.

**What was covered.** In 1998 Bordeaux cardiologist Michel Haïssaguerre reported that in 41 of 45 patients with atrial fibrillation, the stray signals started inside the four veins that bring blood from the lungs<sup class="cite"><a href="#ref-18" title="Source 18">18</a></sup>. The deep dive explained how doctors now wall those veins off, and how a new way of doing it flipped a market in under three years<sup class="cite"><a href="#ref-18" title="Source 18">18</a></sup>.

**How it works.** In atrial fibrillation, the heart's upper chambers quiver at roughly 300 to 600 signals a minute instead of beating in rhythm, like an orchestra drowned out by hecklers in the doorways; blood pools, can clot, and stroke risk rises about fivefold<sup class="cite"><a href="#ref-18" title="Source 18">18</a></sup>.

1. **The fix.** A doctor threads a thin tube (a catheter) from the groin into the heart and scars a ring of tissue around each lung vein, a firebreak the stray signals can't cross; this is called ablation<sup class="cite"><a href="#ref-18" title="Source 18">18</a></sup>.
2. **The old way: heat or cold.** For 25 years the scar was made by heating tissue above about 50°C or freezing it, which occasionally injured the esophagus next door; a hole between heart and esophagus occurred in about 0.025% of cases but killed 66% of those patients<sup class="cite"><a href="#ref-18" title="Source 18">18</a></sup>.
3. **The new way: electric pulses.** Pulsed field ablation fires microsecond bursts that punch holes in cell walls. Heart muscle cells die at a weaker field (0.65 kV/cm in an FDA lab study) than esophagus cells (1.67 kV/cm), so doctors can kill one and spare the other<sup class="cite"><a href="#ref-18" title="Source 18">18</a></sup>.

**Why it matters.** Pulsed field ablation went from zero to about 78% of U.S. ablation catheter spending by early 2026<sup class="cite"><a href="#ref-18" title="Source 18">18</a></sup>. Medtronic overtook first-mover Boston Scientific thanks to Affera, a $925 million startup purchase that combined the catheter with a live 3D map of the heart; its ablation sales grew 58% last quarter<sup class="cite"><a href="#ref-18" title="Source 18">18</a></sup>.

**Test yourself**

1. *Why do the misfires start in the lung veins?* Muscle cells that extend into the pulmonary veins can fire on their own; Haïssaguerre found that is where the trigger sat in 41 of 45 patients, so walling off the veins stops the chaos at its source<sup class="cite"><a href="#ref-18" title="Source 18">18</a></sup>.
2. *What makes pulsed field ablation safer for the esophagus?* Heart muscle dies at a much weaker electric field than esophagus tissue, about 0.65 versus 1.67 kV/cm, so the field can be tuned to kill one and spare the other, while heat spreads to whatever is nearby<sup class="cite"><a href="#ref-18" title="Source 18">18</a></sup>.
3. *Why does owning the mapping system help Medtronic sell more catheters?* Affera's catheter is tied into Affera's own 3D mapping system, so every hospital that installs the map becomes a repeat buyer of Medtronic catheters<sup class="cite"><a href="#ref-18" title="Source 18">18</a></sup>.

### Three days ago: How your tax return travels through the IRS's 1960s computer (Intuit)

*Deep dive covering Sunday, Nov. 22, 2026, in the Monday, Nov. 23 briefing*<sup class="cite"><a href="#ref-17" title="Source 17">17</a></sup>.

**What was covered.** In 2024, IRS Commissioner Danny Werfel described a software project as "preventing a potential collapse" of the system under the tax code: the Individual Master File, the IRS's master record of every taxpayer's account, designed in the late 1960s<sup class="cite"><a href="#ref-17" title="Source 17">17</a></sup>. The deep dive followed one e-filed return from TurboTax to refund<sup class="cite"><a href="#ref-17" title="Source 17">17</a></sup>.

**How it works.** Picture a bank whose front desk checks your deposit slip in minutes but posts every slip to its giant ledger only once a week<sup class="cite"><a href="#ref-17" title="Source 17">17</a></sup>.

1. **Front door.** Intuit, a licensed transmitter, sends your return to the IRS's Modernized e-File gateway, which checks it against business rules (does the Social Security number match the name, was the dependent already claimed) and accepts or rejects it within 24 hours<sup class="cite"><a href="#ref-17" title="Source 17">17</a></sup>.
2. **Fraud screens.** Hundreds of identity-theft filters run before money leaves; by law, refunds with certain family tax credits wait until mid-February<sup class="cite"><a href="#ref-17" title="Source 17">17</a></sup>.
3. **The old ledger.** Posting to your account ran on assembly and COBOL code encoding decades of tax law<sup class="cite"><a href="#ref-17" title="Source 17">17</a></sup>.
4. **The fix.** After failed rewrites starting in 1999, engineers translated the old code into Java piece by piece and ran old and new side by side until they matched 99% of the time<sup class="cite"><a href="#ref-17" title="Source 17">17</a></sup>.

**Why it matters.** In 2026, the first season on the new engine and after deep staff cuts, the IRS had processed only 92.3% of returns received by early February, versus 99.7% a year earlier, before catching up by May<sup class="cite"><a href="#ref-17" title="Source 17">17</a></sup>. Paper returns, which people key in by hand, are where the system breaks<sup class="cite"><a href="#ref-17" title="Source 17">17</a></sup>.

**Test yourself**

1. *Why doesn't "accepted" mean your return has posted?* Acceptance only means the front-door gateway found no rule violations; the return still has to pass fraud screens and be posted to your account in the master file's batch runs<sup class="cite"><a href="#ref-17" title="Source 17">17</a></sup>.
2. *Why did translating code succeed where rewrites failed?* Rewriting from scratch meant rediscovering decades of tax rules buried in old code; translating it line by line kept that logic intact, and running old and new side by side proved the outputs matched<sup class="cite"><a href="#ref-17" title="Source 17">17</a></sup>.
3. *Why are paper returns the weak point?* They need people to key them in by hand, so staff cuts create backlogs, while e-filed returns flow mostly by computer<sup class="cite"><a href="#ref-17" title="Source 17">17</a></sup>.

## Deep dive: How an inkjet printer fires a drop of ink, and HP's fiscal Q4
{: #deep-dive .deep-dive-head}

In 1977, engineers at Canon in Japan were trying to squeeze ink out of a nozzle with a vibrating crystal when someone accidentally touched a syringe full of ink with a hot soldering iron, and ink shot out of the needle<sup class="cite"><a href="#ref-20" title="Source 20">20</a></sup>. Ichiro Endo saw what had happened, and within days his team had a working model of what became the Canon Bubble Jet<sup class="cite"><a href="#ref-19" title="Source 19">19</a></sup>. At almost the same time, half a world away, John Vaught at HP Laboratories was inventing the same trick<sup class="cite"><a href="#ref-19" title="Source 19">19</a>,<a href="#ref-21" title="Source 21">21</a></sup>.

### The short version

A thermal inkjet printer boils ink on purpose: a tiny heater under each nozzle flashes hot for a few millionths of a second, a vapor bubble forms, and the bubble shoves out a drop of ink far smaller than a raindrop, thousands of times a second per nozzle<sup class="cite"><a href="#ref-22" title="Source 22">22</a>,<a href="#ref-20" title="Source 20">20</a></sup>. HP turned that trick into the first mass-market personal inkjet printer in 1984 and into one of the most profitable businesses in technology: selling the printer cheaply and making the money on the ink<sup class="cite"><a href="#ref-23" title="Source 23">23</a>,<a href="#ref-26" title="Source 26">26</a></sup>. Forty years on, that ink business is slowly shrinking, and how fast it shrinks is a big part of HP's story<sup class="cite"><a href="#ref-5" title="Source 5">5</a>,<a href="#ref-7" title="Source 7">7</a></sup>.

### Key terms

<div class="table-wrap" markdown="1">

| Term | Plain meaning |
| --- | --- |
| Thermal inkjet | Printing by flash-boiling a thin layer of ink so a vapor bubble pushes a drop out of a nozzle (Canon calls it Bubble Jet) |
| Printhead | The chip with the heaters, the tiny ink chambers and the row of nozzles; in most HP cartridges it is built into the cartridge and thrown away with it |
| Picoliter | A trillionth of a liter; a modern inkjet drop is about 4-5 of them, far smaller than the eye can see |
| Piezoelectric inkjet | The rival method, used by Epson: a crystal that bends when voltage is applied squeezes the ink out instead of boiling it |
| Kogation | The crust of burned ink residue that builds up on a heater after millions of firings and eventually stops it working |
| Supplies | Printer-industry shorthand for ink and toner cartridges, the part of the business that makes the profit |

</div>

### How it works

**The analogy: a kettle with a hair trigger.** Picture a kettle so small and so fast that it boils, spits and goes quiet in less time than a camera flash, and does it over and over. Each nozzle on an inkjet printhead sits on top of one of these: a tiny chamber of ink with a heater the size of a speck of dust at the bottom. Water normally boils at 100°C, but heat it fast enough and a thin layer can be driven far above that before it boils, which is what makes the trick work.

**How HP found its way there.** At HP Labs, Vaught proposed using a thin-film resistor, the same kind of microscopic heating strip HP was already making for its chips, to boil ink into steam bubbles<sup class="cite"><a href="#ref-21" title="Source 21">21</a></sup>. The first prototype was crude: a single hole punched by hand with a sewing machine needle borrowed from an engineer's wife. It shot fountain pen ink several inches through the air<sup class="cite"><a href="#ref-21" title="Source 21">21</a></sup>. HP's team did not know Canon was working on the same idea, and the two learned of each other's work about two years later<sup class="cite"><a href="#ref-21" title="Source 21">21</a>,<a href="#ref-20" title="Source 20">20</a></sup>. Both men later shared the Optical Society's Edwin H. Land Medal for their independent inventions<sup class="cite"><a href="#ref-19" title="Source 19">19</a></sup>.

**Mechanism one: the 50-microsecond cycle.** HP engineer Christopher Beatty described one firing in 1996<sup class="cite"><a href="#ref-22" title="Source 22">22</a></sup>. An electrical pulse only a few millionths of a second long hits the heater and superheats a layer of ink touching it to about 250°C<sup class="cite"><a href="#ref-22" title="Source 22">22</a></sup>. That layer turns to vapor almost all at once across the heater's surface, forming a single bubble rather than the scattered bubbles of a pot on the stove<sup class="cite"><a href="#ref-22" title="Source 22">22</a></sup>. After 5 microseconds the pulse is over; at 15 microseconds the bubble is fully grown and the drop is leaving the nozzle; at 24 microseconds the bubble collapses back onto the heater<sup class="cite"><a href="#ref-22" title="Source 22">22</a></sup>. Then the chamber refills, pulled in by the ink's surface tension and the shrinking bubble, which takes another 25 to 50 microseconds<sup class="cite"><a href="#ref-22" title="Source 22">22</a>,<a href="#ref-20" title="Source 20">20</a></sup>. Add that up and one nozzle can fire roughly 13,000 to 20,000 times a second (my own arithmetic), in line with the 8-12 kHz typical of heads around 2000 and up to 18 kHz for some<sup class="cite"><a href="#ref-20" title="Source 20">20</a></sup>.

**Mechanism two: shrinking the drop.** HP's 1984 ThinkJet had just 12 nozzles spaced a quarter of a millimeter apart, which limited it to about 96 dots per inch<sup class="cite"><a href="#ref-21" title="Source 21">21</a>,<a href="#ref-22" title="Source 22">22</a></sup>. Each drop weighed about 3 ten-millionths of a gram<sup class="cite"><a href="#ref-21" title="Source 21">21</a></sup>, which works out to about 300 picoliters (my own arithmetic). By 2000, heads fired drops of about 5 picoliters<sup class="cite"><a href="#ref-20" title="Source 20">20</a></sup>, roughly a sixtieth of the ThinkJet's. Smaller drops mean finer dots, which is how inkjets went from blocky letters to photographs. The physics also explains a rule about the ink itself: thermal inks must contain something that vaporizes easily, so they are mostly water-based<sup class="cite"><a href="#ref-20" title="Source 20">20</a></sup>.

**Mechanism three: wearing out on purpose.** Boiling ink millions of times is brutal on the heater. When the bubble collapses it slams a pressure pulse into the thin films<sup class="cite"><a href="#ref-22" title="Source 22">22</a></sup>, and burned residue, kogation, builds up and eventually insulates the heater from the ink<sup class="cite"><a href="#ref-21" title="Source 21">21</a></sup>. HP's engineers made a decision that shaped the whole industry: instead of building a printhead that lasts forever, they built the whole printhead into the cartridge and made it disposable, so every new cartridge brings fresh heaters<sup class="cite"><a href="#ref-21" title="Source 21">21</a></sup>. The ThinkJet's head needed to survive about 2 million firings to print its rated 500 pages, and HP predicted 25 million<sup class="cite"><a href="#ref-21" title="Source 21">21</a></sup>. A later protective layer of tantalum improved head life by more than ten times<sup class="cite"><a href="#ref-22" title="Source 22">22</a></sup>.

**The rival: squeezing instead of boiling.** Epson took the other road. Its printheads put a piezoelectric crystal behind each nozzle; voltage makes the crystal change shape and push a pressure pulse through the ink<sup class="cite"><a href="#ref-20" title="Source 20">20</a></sup>. Because nothing boils, piezo heads can use a wider range of inks and do not suffer kogation, but they cost more to make, so the head stays in the printer<sup class="cite"><a href="#ref-20" title="Source 20">20</a></sup>. Thermal heads are cheaper to produce, which is why HP could afford to throw them away with each cartridge<sup class="cite"><a href="#ref-20" title="Source 20">20</a></sup>.

### Step by step: printing one letter on a 1984 ThinkJet

1. **The computer sends the letter.** The printer's electronics break it into a grid of dots, 12 rows tall for the 12 nozzles<sup class="cite"><a href="#ref-21" title="Source 21">21</a></sup>.
2. **The head moves.** The cartridge slides across the page; at each column, the electronics decide which of the 12 heaters to fire<sup class="cite"><a href="#ref-21" title="Source 21">21</a></sup>.
3. **Fire.** A pulse of about 6 microseconds, which HP chose as the best balance between saving power and tolerating voltage swings, heats each chosen resistor far above ink's boiling point<sup class="cite"><a href="#ref-21" title="Source 21">21</a></sup>.
4. **Bubble and drop.** The vapor bubble shoves a drop out of the nozzle toward the paper; the bubble collapses back onto the heater within microseconds<sup class="cite"><a href="#ref-21" title="Source 21">21</a>,<a href="#ref-22" title="Source 22">22</a></sup>.
5. **Refill and repeat.** Fresh ink flows in from the reservoir and the next column fires; the printer managed about 150 characters per second, faster than the 120 of typical dot-matrix printers, and with a quiet hum instead of a screech<sup class="cite"><a href="#ref-21" title="Source 21">21</a>,<a href="#ref-24" title="Source 24">24</a></sup>.
6. **Throw it away.** After about 500 pages, you replaced the cartridge, ink and printhead together, for $8<sup class="cite"><a href="#ref-24" title="Source 24">24</a></sup>.

### Where things stand

**"The ink model works, and HP is defending it."** Then-CEO Enrique Lores put it plainly at Davos in 2024: "We lose money on the hardware, we make money on the supplies"<sup class="cite"><a href="#ref-26" title="Source 26">26</a></sup>. He also said a customer who doesn't print enough or doesn't buy HP supplies is "a bad investment"<sup class="cite"><a href="#ref-25" title="Source 25">25</a></sup>. To protect the model, HP uses chips in its cartridges and firmware it calls Dynamic Security, which can reject cartridges without an HP security chip; HP says it discloses this and argues that third-party cartridges can pose security risks<sup class="cite"><a href="#ref-25" title="Source 25">25</a>,<a href="#ref-26" title="Source 26">26</a></sup>. The courts have not stopped it: HP settled a toner lockout class action in 2025 without paying, promising to keep disclosing Dynamic Security and to let users decline firmware updates that include it, and an Illinois judge dismissed an inkjet case in September 2025 with leave to amend; the plaintiffs refiled in November 2025<sup class="cite"><a href="#ref-26" title="Source 26">26</a>,<a href="#ref-25" title="Source 25">25</a></sup>. And the decline is slowing: Supplies revenue fell 4% year over year in fiscal Q4 2025, 3% in fiscal Q3 2026 and 2% in fiscal Q4 2026<sup class="cite"><a href="#ref-7" title="Source 7">7</a>,<a href="#ref-6" title="Source 6">6</a>,<a href="#ref-5" title="Source 5">5</a></sup>.

**"The base is shrinking, and customers resent the lock-in."** Every cartridge sale depends on a printer already in someone's home or office, and people are printing less. HP's printer hardware units fell 12% in fiscal Q4 2025 and 7% in fiscal Q3 2026 year over year<sup class="cite"><a href="#ref-7" title="Source 7">7</a>,<a href="#ref-6" title="Source 6">6</a></sup>. Fewer printers sold today means fewer cartridges sold for years afterward. The legal fights keep coming, and critics argue the chips are as much about blocking cheaper ink as about security; The Register says it knows of no case of malware in a cartridge chip outside a lab<sup class="cite"><a href="#ref-26" title="Source 26">26</a></sup>. Researchers have also found that chips can declare a cartridge empty early; in one test of an Epson printer, a researcher printed up to 38% more good pages after defeating the chip<sup class="cite"><a href="#ref-20" title="Source 20">20</a></sup>. Supplies revenue has not grown on a yearly basis in any of the last three quarters shown<sup class="cite"><a href="#ref-7" title="Source 7">7</a>,<a href="#ref-6" title="Source 6">6</a>,<a href="#ref-5" title="Source 5">5</a></sup>.

### Why it matters

- **For technology:** thermal inkjet is one of the great examples of turning a lab accident into precision mass manufacturing; the same drop-on-demand idea is used to print circuits, 3D parts and even biological samples<sup class="cite"><a href="#ref-20" title="Source 20">20</a></sup>.
- **For consumers:** cheap printers and expensive ink mean the cost of printing is hidden in the cartridge, and fights over chips and firmware decide whether cheaper ink is an option<sup class="cite"><a href="#ref-25" title="Source 25">25</a>,<a href="#ref-26" title="Source 26">26</a></sup>.
- **For HP:** printing supplies are a large share of its profit, so a slow decline funds the company while a faster one would squeeze it, just as memory costs pressure its PC margins<sup class="cite"><a href="#ref-5" title="Source 5">5</a></sup>.

### Talking points

1. An inkjet printer boils ink on purpose: a heater flashes to about 250°C for a few microseconds, and the vapor bubble shoves out a drop<sup class="cite"><a href="#ref-22" title="Source 22">22</a></sup>.
2. The whole cycle, from pulse to refill, takes roughly 50 to 75 microseconds, so each nozzle can fire thousands of times a second<sup class="cite"><a href="#ref-22" title="Source 22">22</a>,<a href="#ref-20" title="Source 20">20</a></sup>.
3. Canon and HP invented it independently in the late 1970s; Canon's version started with a soldering iron accidentally touching a syringe of ink<sup class="cite"><a href="#ref-20" title="Source 20">20</a>,<a href="#ref-19" title="Source 19">19</a></sup>.
4. HP made the printhead part of the throwaway cartridge, which solved the wear problem and created the cheap-printer, expensive-ink business model<sup class="cite"><a href="#ref-21" title="Source 21">21</a>,<a href="#ref-26" title="Source 26">26</a></sup>.
5. Epson went the other way, squeezing ink with a bending crystal and keeping the head in the printer<sup class="cite"><a href="#ref-20" title="Source 20">20</a></sup>.
6. Explain it to a friend: HP's stock jumped 11.5% after a beat driven by PCs, but most of its profit still comes from ink, a business that is shrinking a little each year as people print less.
