### One day ago: How a tractor steers itself to the inch and learns to see weeds (Deere)

*Deep dive covering Thursday, Nov. 26, 2026, in the Friday, Nov. 27 briefing*<sup class="cite"><a href="#ref-15" title="Source 15">15</a></sup>

**What was covered.** In the 1830s John Deere turned a saw blade into a steel plow that could cut sticky prairie soil; nearly two centuries later his company sells tractors that steer themselves to within about an inch and sprayers that look at every plant and hit only the weeds<sup class="cite"><a href="#ref-15" title="Source 15">15</a></sup>.

**How it works.** Picture lining a mile-wide soccer pitch, stripe after stripe, without overlaps or gaps, while dabbing weedkiller on every dandelion and missing the grass<sup class="cite"><a href="#ref-15" title="Source 15">15</a></sup>.

1. **Plain GPS is too coarse.** A phone's GPS is typically good to about 4.9 meters (16 feet) under open sky; a tractor's side-by-side passes need inches<sup class="cite"><a href="#ref-15" title="Source 15">15</a></sup>.
2. **Borrow a known point.** A reference receiver at a surveyed spot works out how wrong the satellites are reading and broadcasts the correction to nearby machines (differential correction); Deere's own StarFire system sends corrections down from communications satellites<sup class="cite"><a href="#ref-15" title="Source 15">15</a></sup>.
3. **Count the waves (RTK).** The most precise method measures the satellite's radio wave itself; each wave on the main civilian signal is about 19 cm long, and resolving the count from a nearby base station gives accuracy of about 2.5 cm<sup class="cite"><a href="#ref-15" title="Source 15">15</a></sup>.
4. **Turn the wheels.** Autosteer moves the steering with a hydraulic valve; Iowa State measured about 3.3% overlap in planting and 7% in tillage without guidance<sup class="cite"><a href="#ref-15" title="Source 15">15</a></sup>.
5. **See the weeds.** See & Spray uses cameras on a 120-foot boom and a trained computer model to decide, many times a second, which green patches are weeds, then opens only the nozzles above them<sup class="cite"><a href="#ref-15" title="Source 15">15</a></sup>.

**Why it matters.** Deere reported herbicide savings of 59% in 2024 and nearly 50% (non-residual herbicide) in 2025, but Arkansas research found that a low sensitivity setting let pigweed populations grow 280% a year<sup class="cite"><a href="#ref-15" title="Source 15">15</a></sup>. Deere's big-farm segment sales fell 15% in fiscal Q4, and Deere guides them up 5-15% for fiscal 2027<sup class="cite"><a href="#ref-15" title="Source 15">15</a></sup>.

**Test yourself**

1. *Why can't a tractor rely on plain GPS?* Plain GPS is typically accurate only to about 16 feet, while neighboring passes across a field must line up within inches, so the errors have to be corrected<sup class="cite"><a href="#ref-15" title="Source 15">15</a></sup>.
2. *What does an RTK base station do?* It sits at a known position and rebroadcasts the phase of the satellite signal it sees, letting the tractor resolve the count of radio waves to the satellite and reach roughly centimeter accuracy<sup class="cite"><a href="#ref-15" title="Source 15">15</a></sup>.
3. *What is the trade-off in See & Spray's sensitivity setting?* Lower sensitivity saves more herbicide but misses small weeds, which can then multiply; an Arkansas researcher advised the high-sensitivity setting in most cases<sup class="cite"><a href="#ref-15" title="Source 15">15</a></sup>.

### Three days ago: How doctors calm a quivering heart with electric pulses (Medtronic)

*Deep dive covering Tuesday, Nov. 24, 2026, in the Wednesday, Nov. 25 briefing*<sup class="cite"><a href="#ref-16" title="Source 16">16</a></sup>

**What was covered.** In 1998 Bordeaux cardiologist Michel Haïssaguerre's team reported that in 41 of 45 patients with atrial fibrillation, the misfiring started inside the veins from the lungs; that finding created catheter ablation, now being remade by a method that uses electric pulses instead of heat or cold<sup class="cite"><a href="#ref-16" title="Source 16">16</a></sup>.

**How it works.** A healthy heartbeat is an orchestra following one conductor; in atrial fibrillation (AFib) the upper chambers quiver at roughly 300 to 600 signals a minute, set off by hecklers sitting in the doorways, the four pulmonary veins<sup class="cite"><a href="#ref-16" title="Source 16">16</a></sup>.

1. **Soundproof the doorway.** Doctors thread a catheter (a thin tube) into the heart and scar a ring of tissue around each lung vein so stray signals can't get out (pulmonary vein isolation)<sup class="cite"><a href="#ref-16" title="Source 16">16</a></sup>.
2. **The old way.** Radiofrequency heat or a freezing balloon made the scar, but heat and cold also endangered neighbors such as the esophagus; a worldwide survey found a hole into the esophagus in 0.025% of procedures, and 66% of those patients died<sup class="cite"><a href="#ref-16" title="Source 16">16</a></sup>.
3. **The new way.** Pulsed field ablation fires microsecond electric pulses that punch holes in cell membranes; an FDA lab study found heart muscle cells died at 0.65 kV/cm while esophagus cells needed 1.67 kV/cm, so the field can be tuned to spare the esophagus<sup class="cite"><a href="#ref-16" title="Source 16">16</a></sup>.

**Why it matters.** AFib raises the risk of the most common kind of stroke about five times, and pulsed field ablation went from zero to about 78% of U.S. ablation catheter spending by early 2026; Medtronic's ablation sales grew 58% in its fiscal Q2<sup class="cite"><a href="#ref-16" title="Source 16">16</a></sup>.

**Test yourself**

1. *Where does AFib usually start?* In most patients the stray signals start inside the pulmonary veins, the four veins that carry blood from the lungs into the left upper chamber<sup class="cite"><a href="#ref-16" title="Source 16">16</a></sup>.
2. *Why is pulsed field ablation safer for the esophagus?* Different tissues die at different electric field strengths; heart muscle dies at roughly a third to half the field the esophagus needs, so the pulses can kill one and spare the other<sup class="cite"><a href="#ref-16" title="Source 16">16</a></sup>.
3. *Why is AFib dangerous?* A quivering chamber doesn't empty fully, so blood can pool and clot, and a clot that travels to the brain causes a stroke<sup class="cite"><a href="#ref-16" title="Source 16">16</a></sup>.

## Deep dive: How a computer draws a building, from a light pen to BIM, and Autodesk's fiscal Q3
{: #deep-dive .deep-dive-head}

<div class="dd-nav-slot"></div>


When a graduate student named Ivan Sutherland asked Wesley Clark for time on the TX-2, a computer at MIT's Lincoln Laboratory, Clark gave him access without hesitation, IEEE Spectrum's history recounts<sup class="cite"><a href="#ref-18" title="Source 18">18</a></sup>. The TX-2 had 64k words of memory, and Sutherland used it to build Sketchpad, a program that let a person draw directly on the screen with the recently invented light pen, a pen-shaped sensor pointed at the monitor<sup class="cite"><a href="#ref-17" title="Source 17">17</a></sup>. He finished his PhD on it in January 1963<sup class="cite"><a href="#ref-18" title="Source 18">18</a></sup>. His thesis opened with the sentence: "The Sketchpad system makes it possible for a man and a computer to converse rapidly through the medium of line drawings"<sup class="cite"><a href="#ref-18" title="Source 18">18</a></sup>. Autodesk, whose AutoCAD became the dominant drawing program on personal computers, reported its quarter on Tuesday<sup class="cite"><a href="#ref-20" title="Source 20">20</a>,<a href="#ref-32" title="Source 32">32</a></sup>. Our read: six decades on, that kind of conversation between a person and a computer is part of how many buildings and airplane parts are designed.

### The short version

Computer-aided design (CAD) software doesn't store a picture of a drawing; it stores the geometry itself, as numbers and rules, so a line can be measured, moved or copied exactly<sup class="cite"><a href="#ref-17" title="Source 17">17</a>,<a href="#ref-22" title="Source 22">22</a></sup>. Over four decades the stored object grew from flat lines into 3D solids, then into models that rebuild themselves when one dimension changes, and finally into building information modeling (BIM), where a building is a shared database of real objects such as walls, ducts and beams<sup class="cite"><a href="#ref-22" title="Source 22">22</a>,<a href="#ref-23" title="Source 23">23</a>,<a href="#ref-24" title="Source 24">24</a>,<a href="#ref-26" title="Source 26">26</a></sup>. Our read: one large open question is whether artificial intelligence makes that database more valuable or makes the software that keeps it less necessary.

### Key terms

<div class="table-wrap" markdown="1">

| Term | Plain meaning |
| --- | --- |
| CAD (computer-aided design) | Software for drawing and designing objects precisely, storing exact geometry rather than pixels |
| Control points | A handful of guide points that pull a smooth curve into shape, the way pins hold a bent strip of wood |
| Boundary representation (B-rep) | Describing a solid object by its skin: the faces, edges and corner points that enclose it |
| Parametric model | A model built from dimensions and rules, so changing one number regenerates the whole shape |
| BIM (building information modeling) | A shared digital model of a building made of real objects with data attached, used to design, build and run it |
| Clash detection | Software that checks a combined building model for things in the same place, like a duct running through a beam |
| Generative design | Software that produces many possible designs from goals and limits a person sets, such as strength and weight |

</div>

### How it works

**The analogy: a recipe, not a photograph.** A photo of a cake tells you what it looked like; a recipe tells you how to make it again, bigger, with less sugar. A paint program stores the photo: a grid of colored dots. CAD stores the recipe: a line from this point to that point, a circle of this radius here. Every step of CAD's history has been about writing a richer recipe.

**Mechanism one: storing a drawing as numbers.** Sketchpad's display file used 36-bit words, and of the 36 bits used for each spot on the screen, 20 held its coordinates<sup class="cite"><a href="#ref-17" title="Source 17">17</a></sup>. Because the drawing was coordinates rather than dots, the program could do things paper couldn't. It let users constrain the geometric properties of a drawing<sup class="cite"><a href="#ref-17" title="Source 17">17</a></sup>. And it introduced master drawings that could be copied into many instances; change the master, and every copy changed with it<sup class="cite"><a href="#ref-17" title="Source 17">17</a></sup>. Wikipedia calls Sketchpad the ancestor of modern CAD programs, and says it also inspired the graphical user interface and object-oriented programming<sup class="cite"><a href="#ref-17" title="Source 17">17</a></sup>.

The recipe moved onto ordinary desks in the 1980s. In 1982 John Walker and 12 other programmers pooled $59,000 to start Autodesk, and the first product they finished was AutoCAD, which began life as a CAD program called Interact written by Michael Riddle<sup class="cite"><a href="#ref-19" title="Source 19">19</a></sup>. AutoCAD became the dominant CAD program on personal computers, according to the Computer History Museum<sup class="cite"><a href="#ref-20" title="Source 20">20</a></sup>.

**Mechanism two: how a computer draws a smooth curve.** Straight lines are easy to store; a car fender is not. In 1959 Paul de Casteljau worked out a mathematical way to build smooth curves, and Pierre Bézier, an engineer at Renault from 1933 to 1975, popularized the method in his UNISURF design system, which debuted in 1968<sup class="cite"><a href="#ref-21" title="Source 21">21</a></sup>. The idea behind these Bézier curves: instead of storing thousands of points along the curve, you store a few control points. The curve starts at the first point and ends at the last, and the points in between act like magnets, pulling the curve toward them without the curve passing through them. Drag one control point and the whole curve bends smoothly.

**Mechanism three: from lines to solids.** A drawing made of lines can't tell you the weight of a part, or whether two parts collide. For that the computer needs to know what is inside and what is outside. The common answer is boundary representation: a solid is stored as a collection of connected surface pieces, where a face is a bounded portion of a surface, an edge is a bounded piece of a curve and a vertex sits at a point<sup class="cite"><a href="#ref-22" title="Source 22">22</a></sup>. The main alternative, constructive solid geometry, builds shapes by adding and subtracting simple blocks, cylinders and spheres; boundary representation is the more flexible of the two, Wikipedia notes<sup class="cite"><a href="#ref-22" title="Source 22">22</a></sup>. Think of an egg: B-rep describes the shell precisely, and the computer infers that everything inside the shell is egg.

**Mechanism four: models that rebuild themselves.** The next leap came from a company called PTC. Its Pro/Engineer succeeded, engineering.com writes, because it built 3D models parametrically, feature by feature<sup class="cite"><a href="#ref-23" title="Source 23">23</a></sup>. The model is a recipe of steps (sketch a rectangle 80 mm wide, pull it up 40 mm, drill a hole 10 mm from the edge), each tied to dimensions. Change a dimension and the software replays the recipe from its history tree; engineering.com's example is altering an engine model's displacement, or even its number of cylinders, by changing a few parameters<sup class="cite"><a href="#ref-23" title="Source 23">23</a></sup>. The first two seats went to a familiar name from yesterday's deep dive: engineering.com dates the modern era of 3D CAD to September 1987, when Deere & Company bought the first two seats of Pro/Engineer<sup class="cite"><a href="#ref-23" title="Source 23">23</a></sup>. The weakness is the same recipe logic: if a parent step is deleted or changed in certain ways, everything built on it can break in unexpected ways, and sometimes users had to rebuild the model from scratch<sup class="cite"><a href="#ref-23" title="Source 23">23</a></sup>.

**Mechanism five: a building as a database.** On Oct. 31, 1997, in Newton, Massachusetts, two key developers of Pro/Engineer, Leonid Raiz and Irwin Jungreis, founded Charles River Software<sup class="cite"><a href="#ref-24" title="Source 24">24</a></sup>. Both saw building and construction as a field that could gain from design computing<sup class="cite"><a href="#ref-25" title="Source 25">25</a></sup>. In concept testing, one firm told them, "I don't think you can pull this off, but if you could do this, it would be the Holy Grail"<sup class="cite"><a href="#ref-25" title="Source 25">25</a></sup>. Their product shipped as version 1.0 on April 5, 2000, and the company was renamed after it: the ease of making changes inspired the name Revit, short for "Revise-Instantly"<sup class="cite"><a href="#ref-24" title="Source 24">24</a></sup>. At its core was a change engine that let any edit spread automatically to keep the whole model consistent<sup class="cite"><a href="#ref-24" title="Source 24">24</a></sup>. Move a wall in the floor plan, and the elevations, sections, door schedules and area counts all update, because they are views of one model, not separate drawings. Autodesk bought the company for $133 million in 2002<sup class="cite"><a href="#ref-24" title="Source 24">24</a></sup>.

That model is what building information modeling means. The ISO 19650 standard defines it as the use of a shared digital representation of a building to support its design, construction and operation<sup class="cite"><a href="#ref-26" title="Source 26">26</a></sup>. The objects carry data, not just shape: information about floors, spaces, systems and components<sup class="cite"><a href="#ref-26" title="Source 26">26</a></sup>. And, echoing Sketchpad's masters, each object is defined only once and then placed in the model wherever it is needed<sup class="cite"><a href="#ref-26" title="Source 26">26</a></sup>. Governments noticed. The UK made Level 2 BIM, a managed 3D environment with data attached, a minimum requirement on centrally procured public projects from April 2016<sup class="cite"><a href="#ref-26" title="Source 26">26</a></sup>.

**Mechanism six: catching mistakes before they're built.** On a real project the architect, the structural engineer and the mechanical engineer each build their own model. Combine them and the software can check, object by object, whether anything occupies the same space: a duct through a steel beam, a pipe through a column. Each hit is a clash. The builder Haskell ran the numbers on a $230 million design-build food plant in California: its team sorted through about 2,000 clashes, filtered them to about 932 for analysis, and reported $2.22 million in rework savings plus an estimated $542,000 in schedule savings, a 10-times return on the labor spent finding them<sup class="cite"><a href="#ref-27" title="Source 27">27</a></sup>.

**Mechanism seven: letting the computer propose the shape.** Generative design turns the process around: the designer sets goals and limits, and the software generates thousands of alternatives that meet them<sup class="cite"><a href="#ref-28" title="Source 28">28</a></sup>. Airbus and Autodesk used it on a partition wall for the A320 cabin; the result, 3D-printed in an aluminum-magnesium-scandium alloy called Scalmalloy, mimics cellular structures and bone growth found in living things and weighs 45% (30 kg) less than the existing design, per Airbus<sup class="cite"><a href="#ref-28" title="Source 28">28</a></sup>. Airbus estimated that the new design approach could save up to 465,000 metric tonnes of CO2 emissions a year, since less weight means less fuel<sup class="cite"><a href="#ref-28" title="Source 28">28</a></sup>. Autodesk's research group describes the part as almost 50% lighter and notes it was undergoing crash testing<sup class="cite"><a href="#ref-29" title="Source 29">29</a></sup>.

### Step by step: one hospital corridor, from idea to job site

1. **Draw with objects.** The architect places walls, doors and ceilings in a BIM model; each is an object with dimensions and data, defined once and reused<sup class="cite"><a href="#ref-26" title="Source 26">26</a></sup>.
2. **Link the trades.** The structural engineer's beams and the mechanical engineer's ducts and pipes are combined with the architect's model in one shared environment<sup class="cite"><a href="#ref-26" title="Source 26">26</a></sup>.
3. **Run the clash check.** The software flags every place where two objects collide; each one is a clash for the team to sort and resolve<sup class="cite"><a href="#ref-27" title="Source 27">27</a></sup>.
4. **Fix it on screen.** The team moves a duct or resizes a beam, and the change propagates through the parametric model to keep it consistent<sup class="cite"><a href="#ref-24" title="Source 24">24</a></sup>.
5. **Build and run from the model.** The same shared model supports construction and, after handover, operation of the building<sup class="cite"><a href="#ref-26" title="Source 26">26</a></sup>.

### Why it's hard, and what limits it

**Fragile recipes.** The same history tree that makes parametric models powerful makes them brittle: change an early step the wrong way and later steps fail, and researcher Jami Shah, quoted by engineering.com, wrote that there are no universally applicable methods for checking the validity of features<sup class="cite"><a href="#ref-23" title="Source 23">23</a></sup>.

**Customers pushing back.** In July 2020, 25 design firms, including Zaha Hadid Architects and Grimshaw, backed an open letter to Autodesk; 17 signed publicly, which The Architect's Newspaper attributed to fear of retribution<sup class="cite"><a href="#ref-30" title="Source 30">30</a></sup>. The firms said their costs of owning Revit had risen as much as 70% over five years through 2019 while development had stalled, and they had spent $22 million on Autodesk products over that time<sup class="cite"><a href="#ref-30" title="Source 30">30</a>,<a href="#ref-31" title="Source 31">31</a></sup>. The letter said Revit was once the industry's enabler but "increasingly finds itself a constraint and bottleneck"<sup class="cite"><a href="#ref-30" title="Source 30">30</a></sup>. An Autodesk spokesperson told ENR the company was "currently reviewing its contents to inform our response"<sup class="cite"><a href="#ref-31" title="Source 31">31</a></sup>.

**The AI question.** Autodesk's own quarterly release lists the market's reaction to disruption from artificial intelligence among its risks<sup class="cite"><a href="#ref-32" title="Source 32">32</a></sup>. In the same release, CEO Andrew Anagnost said that as AI becomes more capable and accessible, "differentiation will increasingly come from the ability to combine intelligence with trusted engineering capabilities, rich project context and complex industry workflows"<sup class="cite"><a href="#ref-32" title="Source 32">32</a></sup>. Our read: Autodesk appears to be betting that whoever holds the detailed building model also holds context an AI would need, which could make the model more valuable rather than less.

### The quarter in brief: Autodesk

Autodesk's fiscal Q3 2027 (August-October 2026) revenue rose 17% from a year earlier to $2.160 billion (14% in constant currency), above its own guidance of $2.125-2.140 billion<sup class="cite"><a href="#ref-32" title="Source 32">32</a>,<a href="#ref-33" title="Source 33">33</a></sup>. The architecture, engineering and construction (AEC) product family, which includes Revit, grew 19% to $1.097 billion; AutoCAD rose 11% to $525 million and Manufacturing 13% to $403 million<sup class="cite"><a href="#ref-32" title="Source 32">32</a></sup>. Non-GAAP EPS was $3.20, up $0.53 from a year earlier; GAAP EPS of $2.68 included a tax benefit of about $105 million<sup class="cite"><a href="#ref-32" title="Source 32">32</a></sup>. Guidance, raised: fiscal 2027 revenue to $8.360-8.375 billion from $8.295-8.345 billion, and non-GAAP EPS to $12.70-12.76 from $12.52-12.60<sup class="cite"><a href="#ref-32" title="Source 32">32</a>,<a href="#ref-33" title="Source 33">33</a></sup>. The stock, which closed at $255.70 on Tuesday before the report, opened Wednesday at $244.65 (-4.3%) and closed at $246.79 (-3.5%), then rose 2.1% on Friday (my own arithmetic)<sup class="cite"><a href="#ref-3" title="Source 3">3</a></sup>. Caution: the outlook includes MaintainX, a company Autodesk acquired, so not all of the growth comes from the existing business<sup class="cite"><a href="#ref-32" title="Source 32">32</a>,<a href="#ref-33" title="Source 33">33</a></sup>.

### Why it matters

- **For builders:** Haskell measured a 10-times return on the labor spent checking for clashes on one plant<sup class="cite"><a href="#ref-27" title="Source 27">27</a></sup>. Our read: a clash found on screen is usually far cheaper to fix than the same clash found on site, where it can mean tearing out finished work.
- **For design:** generative design lets software search thousands of shapes for one that is lighter or stronger, as the Airbus partition showed<sup class="cite"><a href="#ref-28" title="Source 28">28</a></sup>.
- **For Autodesk:** AEC was about 51% of fiscal Q3 revenue (my own arithmetic) and grew faster than AutoCAD or Manufacturing<sup class="cite"><a href="#ref-32" title="Source 32">32</a></sup>. Our read: whether AI strengthens or weakens the value of owning the building model is one long-run question for the stock.

### Talking points

1. In 1963 Ivan Sutherland drew on a screen with a light pen, and his Sketchpad program could copy a master shape so that changing the master changed every copy<sup class="cite"><a href="#ref-17" title="Source 17">17</a>,<a href="#ref-18" title="Source 18">18</a></sup>.
2. CAD stores the recipe, not the photo: lines and curves as numbers, and smooth curves as a few control points that pull the curve into shape<sup class="cite"><a href="#ref-17" title="Source 17">17</a>,<a href="#ref-21" title="Source 21">21</a></sup>.
3. Deere bought the first two seats of Pro/Engineer, the parametric program engineering.com credits with starting the modern era of 3D CAD, in 1987<sup class="cite"><a href="#ref-23" title="Source 23">23</a></sup>.
4. Revit is short for "Revise-Instantly": move a wall once and every drawing of it updates<sup class="cite"><a href="#ref-24" title="Source 24">24</a></sup>.
5. On one $230 million plant, checking the combined model for clashes saved an estimated $2.55 million net<sup class="cite"><a href="#ref-27" title="Source 27">27</a></sup>.
6. Explain it to a friend: Autodesk sells the software that turns buildings into shared 3D databases, and its revenue grew 17% last quarter, led by its architecture, engineering and construction business, and it lists the market's reaction to AI disruption among its risks<sup class="cite"><a href="#ref-32" title="Source 32">32</a></sup>.
