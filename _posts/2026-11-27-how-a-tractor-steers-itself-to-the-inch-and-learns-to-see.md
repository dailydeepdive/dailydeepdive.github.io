### One day ago: How an inkjet printer fires a drop of ink (HP Inc.)

*Deep dive covering Wednesday, Nov. 25, 2026, in the Thursday, Nov. 26 briefing*<sup class="cite"><a href="#ref-13" title="Source 13">13</a></sup>

**What was covered.** In 1977 a Canon engineer's hot soldering iron touched a syringe of ink and ink shot out of the needle; Ichiro Endo turned that accident into the Bubble Jet, while John Vaught at HP Labs invented the same trick independently<sup class="cite"><a href="#ref-13" title="Source 13">13</a></sup>. The deep dive explained how a thermal inkjet works and why the cartridge, not the printer, is where the money is made<sup class="cite"><a href="#ref-13" title="Source 13">13</a></sup>.

**How it works.** Picture a kettle so small and fast that it boils, spits and goes quiet in less time than a camera flash<sup class="cite"><a href="#ref-13" title="Source 13">13</a></sup>.

1. **Heat.** A pulse a few millionths of a second long hits a microscopic heater under each nozzle and superheats a thin layer of ink to about 250°C<sup class="cite"><a href="#ref-13" title="Source 13">13</a></sup>.
2. **Bubble.** That layer flashes into a single vapor bubble, fully grown about 15 microseconds in, which shoves a drop out of the nozzle<sup class="cite"><a href="#ref-13" title="Source 13">13</a></sup>.
3. **Collapse and refill.** The bubble collapses at about 24 microseconds and fresh ink refills the chamber, so each nozzle can fire thousands of times a second<sup class="cite"><a href="#ref-13" title="Source 13">13</a></sup>.
4. **Wear.** Burned residue (kogation) slowly crusts the heater, so HP built the printhead into the throwaway cartridge, which also created the cheap-printer, expensive-ink model<sup class="cite"><a href="#ref-13" title="Source 13">13</a></sup>.

The rival, Epson's piezoelectric head, squeezes ink with a crystal that bends under voltage instead of boiling it, so the head can last and stay in the printer<sup class="cite"><a href="#ref-13" title="Source 13">13</a></sup>.

**Why it matters.** Drops shrank from about 300 picoliters on HP's 1984 ThinkJet to about 5 picoliters by 2000, which took inkjets from blocky letters to photos<sup class="cite"><a href="#ref-13" title="Source 13">13</a></sup>. For HP, ink and toner (Supplies) remain a large share of profit; the corrected Supplies series ran -4%, -1%, +1%, -3% and -2% year over year from fiscal Q4 2025 through fiscal Q4 2026<sup class="cite"><a href="#ref-13" title="Source 13">13</a></sup>.

**Test yourself**

1. *Why does a thermal inkjet need ink that vaporizes easily?* The drop is pushed out by a vapor bubble made by flash-boiling a thin layer of ink, so thermal inks are mostly water-based<sup class="cite"><a href="#ref-13" title="Source 13">13</a></sup>.
2. *Why did HP make the printhead disposable?* Millions of firings wear the heater down with burned residue, so putting fresh heaters in every new cartridge solved the wear problem<sup class="cite"><a href="#ref-13" title="Source 13">13</a></sup>.
3. *How does Epson's approach differ?* A piezoelectric crystal behind each nozzle bends to push the ink, so nothing boils and the head can stay in the printer, though it costs more to make<sup class="cite"><a href="#ref-13" title="Source 13">13</a></sup>.

### Three days ago: How a grid battery works, and why one burned (Fluence Energy)

*Deep dive covering Monday, Nov. 23, 2026, in the Tuesday, Nov. 24 briefing*<sup class="cite"><a href="#ref-14" title="Source 14">14</a></sup>

**What was covered.** On Jan. 16, 2025, one of the world's biggest batteries, inside a converted power-plant hall at Moss Landing, California, caught fire<sup class="cite"><a href="#ref-14" title="Source 14">14</a></sup>. The deep dive explained what grid batteries do, how they are built and why they sometimes fail<sup class="cite"><a href="#ref-14" title="Source 14">14</a></sup>.

**How it works.** A grid battery is a water tower for electricity: fill it with cheap midday solar power, drain it at the early-evening peak<sup class="cite"><a href="#ref-14" title="Source 14">14</a></sup>.

1. **Two sizes.** Megawatts measure how fast power flows (the pipe); megawatt-hours measure how much is stored (the tank). A 100 MW / 400 MWh battery runs flat out for four hours<sup class="cite"><a href="#ref-14" title="Source 14">14</a></sup>.
2. **Nesting boxes.** Cells make modules, modules stack into racks, racks sit in containers; an inverter converts the battery's direct current to the grid's alternating current<sup class="cite"><a href="#ref-14" title="Source 14">14</a></sup>.
3. **The round-trip tax.** About 15% of the energy is lost as heat on the way in and out, so the battery profits only when evening power costs enough more than midday power<sup class="cite"><a href="#ref-14" title="Source 14">14</a></sup>.
4. **Why fires spread.** In thermal runaway, a cell heats past its tipping point (about 160°C for NMC chemistry, 230°C for the cooler LFP) and sets off its neighbors<sup class="cite"><a href="#ref-14" title="Source 14">14</a></sup>.

**Why it matters.** California's batteries reached 21,112 MW by August 2026, about a third of the state's record peak demand, and Moss Landing burned again in September 2026 with its cause still unknown<sup class="cite"><a href="#ref-14" title="Source 14">14</a></sup>. Fluence took a record $3.5 billion of orders in its quarter while losing money because its own Houston factory fell behind<sup class="cite"><a href="#ref-14" title="Source 14">14</a></sup>.

**Test yourself**

1. *What is the difference between MW and MWh?* MW is the rate power can flow; MWh is the amount stored, so dividing MWh by MW gives how many hours the battery can run at full power<sup class="cite"><a href="#ref-14" title="Source 14">14</a></sup>.
2. *Why does LFP fail more gently than NMC?* Its tipping point for thermal runaway is higher, about 230°C versus about 160°C, so it takes more heat to start the chain reaction<sup class="cite"><a href="#ref-14" title="Source 14">14</a></sup>.
3. *Why must a battery earn a price spread?* About 15% of the energy is lost on the round trip, so it only pays when power sold in the evening is worth enough more than power bought at midday<sup class="cite"><a href="#ref-14" title="Source 14">14</a></sup>.

## Deep dive: How a tractor steers itself to the inch and learns to see weeds, and Deere's fiscal Q4
{: #deep-dive .deep-dive-head}

The farmers of the Illinois prairie had a problem in the 1830s: their cast-iron plows were "not working very well in the tough prairie soil"<sup class="cite"><a href="#ref-15" title="Source 15">15</a></sup>, which the Smithsonian describes as "the rich, sticky, black almost-mud of the Midwestern prairies"<sup class="cite"><a href="#ref-16" title="Source 16">16</a></sup>. In his blacksmith shop in Grand Detour, Illinois, John Deere "took a large circular saw blade, cut off the teeth and reshaped the metal into a curving parallelogram"<sup class="cite"><a href="#ref-16" title="Source 16">16</a></sup>. The result, in the Smithsonian's words: "it scoured"<sup class="cite"><a href="#ref-16" title="Source 16">16</a></sup>. Wikipedia dates the first commercially successful plow to 1837; the Smithsonian places it in 1838<sup class="cite"><a href="#ref-15" title="Source 15">15</a>,<a href="#ref-16" title="Source 16">16</a></sup>. Nearly two centuries later, the company he founded is trying to solve a different farm problem with a different tool: making a giant machine drive a line straight to within an inch, and making a sprayer that looks at every plant and only hits the weeds.

### The short version

A modern tractor knows where it is by listening to GPS satellites, then correcting their small errors with signals from a reference receiver whose exact position is known; with the best corrections it can be accurate to as close as about 2.5 centimeters, roughly an inch<sup class="cite"><a href="#ref-17" title="Source 17">17</a>,<a href="#ref-28" title="Source 28">28</a></sup>. A hydraulic valve then steers the machine along that path without the driver touching the wheel<sup class="cite"><a href="#ref-18" title="Source 18">18</a></sup>. Deere's See & Spray adds eyes: cameras on the spray boom photograph the ground, a trained computer model decides in milliseconds which green things are weeds, and only the nozzles over those weeds open<sup class="cite"><a href="#ref-20" title="Source 20">20</a>,<a href="#ref-36" title="Source 36">36</a></sup>. The open question is whether farmers coming out of a downturn will pay for all of it.

### Key terms

<div class="table-wrap" markdown="1">

| Term | Plain meaning |
| --- | --- |
| GPS | The U.S. network of satellites whose radio signals let a receiver work out where it is |
| Differential correction | Fixing GPS errors using a fixed receiver whose true position is known and that broadcasts how far off the satellites are reading |

| Autosteer | A system that turns the tractor's wheels automatically to follow a planned GPS line |
| Pass-to-pass accuracy | How closely one trip across the field lines up with the trip beside it |
| Green-on-brown vs green-on-green | Spotting green weeds on bare brown soil (easy) versus spotting green weeds among a green crop (hard) |

### How it works

**The analogy: lining a soccer pitch.** Picture painting the lines on a pitch with a wheeled chalk liner, except the pitch is a mile wide and you have to paint the next stripe exactly alongside the last one, all day, without overlapping or leaving gaps. Then imagine you also have to find every dandelion on the pitch and dab weedkiller on it, without wetting the grass. That is the farm problem: hundreds of side-by-side passes, each one costing seed, fuel, fertilizer and chemicals wherever it overlaps.

**Mechanism one: why plain GPS isn't good enough.** GPS has 24 to 32 satellites orbiting about 20,200 kilometers up<sup class="cite"><a href="#ref-25" title="Source 25">25</a></sup>. Each broadcasts its position and the exact time; a receiver measures how long each signal took to arrive, and with at least four satellites in view it can solve for its own position and its clock error<sup class="cite"><a href="#ref-25" title="Source 25">25</a></sup>. That works well for a phone, which is "typically accurate to within a 4.9 m (16 ft.) radius under open sky"<sup class="cite"><a href="#ref-26" title="Source 26">26</a></sup>. For a machine whose passes must line up within inches, 16 feet is far too coarse, and GPS.gov notes that "many things can degrade GPS positioning accuracy"<sup class="cite"><a href="#ref-26" title="Source 26">26</a></sup>.

**Mechanism two: borrowing a known point.** The fix is to park a receiver at a spot whose position is surveyed exactly. That reference station "calculates the difference between its highly accurate known position" and what the satellites say, and sends the correction out by radio; moving receivers nearby "correct their position by the same amount"<sup class="cite"><a href="#ref-27" title="Source 27">27</a></sup>. The catch is that the error "grows approximately linearly with respect to the distance from the broadcast site"<sup class="cite"><a href="#ref-27" title="Source 27">27</a></sup>.

Deere built its own version. StarFire "came about after a meeting in 1994 among John Deere engineers" and was "first offered in 1998"<sup class="cite"><a href="#ref-29" title="Source 29">29</a></sup>. Instead of a radio mast on every farm, it sends corrections down from Inmarsat communications satellites, at a modest "300 bits per second, repeating once a second"<sup class="cite"><a href="#ref-29" title="Source 29">29</a></sup>. The first signal, SF1, was good to about 1 meter; SF2, introduced in 2004, to about 4.5 centimeters (both are "1-sigma" figures, meaning roughly two-thirds of readings fall within that distance)<sup class="cite"><a href="#ref-29" title="Source 29">29</a></sup>. When Deere introduced SF3 in 2016, it advertised "in-season repeatable accuracy to 1.2 inches"<sup class="cite"><a href="#ref-29" title="Source 29">29</a>,<a href="#ref-30" title="Source 30">30</a></sup>.

**Mechanism three: counting the waves (RTK).** The best correction method reads the radio wave itself. A GPS signal is a carrier wave with a coded message riding on it; RTK "uses measurements of the phase of the signal's carrier wave in addition to the information content"<sup class="cite"><a href="#ref-28" title="Source 28">28</a></sup>. Think of measuring a distance by counting waves on a rope: if you know how many whole waves lie between you and the satellite, and where along the last wave you sit, you know the distance to a fraction of a wave. On the main civilian signal, called L1, miscounting by a single wave produces an error of 19 cm<sup class="cite"><a href="#ref-28" title="Source 28">28</a></sup>; in other words, each wave is about 19 centimeters long. A nearby base station "re-broadcasts the phase of the carrier that it observes," usually through a radio modem or over the internet, so the tractor can resolve the count<sup class="cite"><a href="#ref-28" title="Source 28">28</a></sup>. Done right, a single base station gives "accuracy of 8 mm + 1 ppm" horizontally, meaning 8 millimeters plus 1 millimeter for every kilometer of distance, and works out to about 20 km<sup class="cite"><a href="#ref-28" title="Source 28">28</a></sup>. Deere's top receiver quotes "up to 2.5-cm accuracy" with its RTK service<sup class="cite"><a href="#ref-17" title="Source 17">17</a></sup>.

**Mechanism four: turning the wheels.** Knowing where you are is half the job. On a factory autosteer system, "a control valve is used to properly direct hydraulic oil when a steering adjustment needs to be made"; on other machines, "the steering wheel can be replaced with an actuator module that includes its own steering wheel"<sup class="cite"><a href="#ref-18" title="Source 18">18</a></sup>. Why bother? Iowa State engineers measured farmers without guidance and found "an average 3.3 percent overlap during planting operations" and 7% during spring tillage<sup class="cite"><a href="#ref-34" title="Source 34">34</a></sup>. Deere says manual operators "tend to overlap 5 to 13 percent over the course of an entire day"<sup class="cite"><a href="#ref-35" title="Source 35">35</a></sup>. Kansas State extension engineers rate RTK as "capable of 3- to 4-inch overlap control" and say a well-working guidance system "can increase field efficiencies up to 10 percent"<sup class="cite"><a href="#ref-33" title="Source 33">33</a></sup>. The USDA found autosteer and guidance on 58.4% of U.S. corn acres in 2016, 55.9% of winter wheat acres in 2017, 54.5% of soybean acres in 2018 and 64.5% of cotton acres in 2019<sup class="cite"><a href="#ref-44" title="Source 44">44</a></sup>.

**Mechanism five: teaching a sprayer to see.** Steering solves the stripes; it doesn't find the dandelions. In 2011, two Stanford graduates, Jorge Heraud and Lee Redden, founded Blue River Technology in Sunnyvale, California, to build "weed elimination robots for agriculture"<sup class="cite"><a href="#ref-31" title="Source 31">31</a>,<a href="#ref-32" title="Source 32">32</a></sup>. In 2017, Deere agreed to "spend $305 million to acquire" the startup, whose See & Spray equipment "uses computer vision and machine learning to help growers reduce the use of herbicides"<sup class="cite"><a href="#ref-32" title="Source 32">32</a></sup>.

The system learns the way a child learns to tell a dog from a cat: by seeing many labeled examples. Blue River "consulted with professional agronomists and weed scientists on labeling weeds correctly," and "Each frame captured by the camera is analyzed by a PyTorch-enabled neural network," a computer model trained on those labeled pictures; "in a matter of milliseconds" the machine then sprays only where weeds were found<sup class="cite"><a href="#ref-36" title="Source 36">36</a></sup>. Deere launched the easier version first. See & Spray Select, launched in 2021<sup class="cite"><a href="#ref-19" title="Source 19">19</a></sup>, works on fallow fields, where any green thing on brown soil is a weed, and can spot "weeds as small at 1/4-inch diameter"<sup class="cite"><a href="#ref-19" title="Source 19">19</a></sup>. Deere said it used "77% less herbicide on average" with a hit rate similar to spraying everything<sup class="cite"><a href="#ref-19" title="Source 19">19</a></sup>.

The hard version, See & Spray Ultimate, came in 2022: "green on green" spraying in growing corn, cotton and soybeans, where the computer must tell a young weed from a young crop plant<sup class="cite"><a href="#ref-20" title="Source 20">20</a></sup>. It "uses 36 cameras mounted every 3.28 feet across the 120-foot carbon-fiber truss-style boom," and "10 CPUs mounted on the boom" generate "nearly four gigabytes of data per second," scanning "more than 2,100 square feet per second" at up to 12 miles per hour<sup class="cite"><a href="#ref-20" title="Source 20">20</a></sup>. Mississippi State's extension service describes the identification happening "approximately 20 times per second" and herbicide landing in "approximately 4-inch squares across the width of the spray boom"<sup class="cite"><a href="#ref-37" title="Source 37">37</a></sup>. By 2025, Deere said the system could scan "over 2,500 square feet per second at up to 15 miles-per-hour"<sup class="cite"><a href="#ref-39" title="Source 39">39</a></sup>.

### Step by step: one pass of See & Spray Ultimate through a soybean field

1. **Know where you are.** The receiver fixes the sprayer's position, corrected to about an inch, and autosteer holds the planned line<sup class="cite"><a href="#ref-17" title="Source 17">17</a>,<a href="#ref-18" title="Source 18">18</a></sup>.
2. **Look.** Thirty-six cameras along the 120-foot boom photograph the strip of field ahead of the nozzles<sup class="cite"><a href="#ref-20" title="Source 20">20</a></sup>.
3. **Decide.** Processors on the boom run each frame through the trained model, which labels each green patch as crop or weed, about 20 times a second<sup class="cite"><a href="#ref-20" title="Source 20">20</a>,<a href="#ref-37" title="Source 37">37</a></sup>.
4. **Fire.** For each weed, "a command is sent to the proper nozzle to turn on or off," so herbicide lands in a patch about 4 inches square<sup class="cite"><a href="#ref-20" title="Source 20">20</a>,<a href="#ref-37" title="Source 37">37</a></sup>.
5. **Record.** The machine logs where it sprayed; in 2024 Deere said customers saved an estimated 8 million gallons of herbicide mix on more than 1 million acres, an average saving of 59% on corn, soybean and cotton fields<sup class="cite"><a href="#ref-38" title="Source 38">38</a></sup>.

### Why it's hard, and what limits it

**Seeing small weeds versus saving chemical.** The sprayer has a sensitivity dial, and it cuts both ways. University of Arkansas researchers found that running at a low sensitivity setting "increased the Palmer amaranth pigweed population by 280 percent each year"<sup class="cite"><a href="#ref-41" title="Source 41">41</a></sup>; the extension service's fact sheet explains that "lower sensitivities will improve savings, but small weeds will be missed"<sup class="cite"><a href="#ref-40" title="Source 40">40</a></sup>. Arkansas researcher Jason Norsworthy's advice: "All of my data would indicate that using the high-sensitivity setting is best in most scenarios"<sup class="cite"><a href="#ref-41" title="Source 41">41</a></sup>. In 2025, with use spreading to "more than five million acres," Deere put the average reduction in non-residual herbicide at "nearly 50%"<sup class="cite"><a href="#ref-39" title="Source 39">39</a></sup>; for 2024, Deere had reported "an average herbicide savings of 59%" on corn, soybean and cotton fields<sup class="cite"><a href="#ref-38" title="Source 38">38</a></sup>.

**Cost.** Arkansas extension economists list a per-acre usage fee of "$5/acre for crops and $1/acre in fallow," and say an upgrade to an existing sprayer "can cost between $25,000 and $100,000"<sup class="cite"><a href="#ref-40" title="Source 40">40</a></sup>.

**Connectivity.** Deere says "30% of U.S. cropland lacks suitable connectivity"<sup class="cite"><a href="#ref-42" title="Source 42">42</a></sup>, and USDA's 2025 survey found that 85 percent of farms reported having internet access<sup class="cite"><a href="#ref-43" title="Source 43">43</a></sup>; in 2024 Deere announced a plan to deliver satellite internet to machines through Starlink<sup class="cite"><a href="#ref-42" title="Source 42">42</a></sup>.

**Who gets to fix it.** Our read: the more software a tractor carries, the more a repair depends on the maker's tools. In January 2025 the Federal Trade Commission sued Deere, alleging its practices "limited the ability of farmers and independent repair providers to repair Deere equipment"; Deere said it would "vigorously defend itself against this baseless lawsuit"<sup class="cite"><a href="#ref-45" title="Source 45">45</a></sup>. In July 2026 the FTC and five states announced a settlement requiring Deere to make repair resources available "on fair and reasonable terms," under an order lasting 10 years<sup class="cite"><a href="#ref-46" title="Source 46">46</a></sup>.

**Full autonomy.** At CES 2022, Deere showed a driverless 8R tractor with "six pairs of stereo cameras" whose images pass "through a deep neural network that classifies each pixel"<sup class="cite"><a href="#ref-21" title="Source 21">21</a></sup>. Its second-generation kit has 16 cameras for a 360-degree view, with a full launch planned for 2026<sup class="cite"><a href="#ref-22" title="Source 22">22</a>,<a href="#ref-23" title="Source 23">23</a></sup>. At CES in January 2026, Deere executive Aaron Wetzel said, "It may not be fully autonomous, because we still need some folks in the cab, but we're very much on the path to get there"<sup class="cite"><a href="#ref-24" title="Source 24">24</a></sup>.

### The quarter in brief: Deere

Deere's fiscal Q4 (ended Nov. 1, 2026) net sales and revenues fell 2% from a year earlier to $12.115 billion, and EPS was $3.89 versus $3.93 a year earlier (fiscal Q4 2025)<sup class="cite"><a href="#ref-47" title="Source 47">47</a></sup>. ### Why it matters

- **For farming:** Our read: an inch of steering accuracy and a weed-by-weed sprayer turn the same land, seed and chemicals into more output with less waste; the herbicide savings Deere reports for 2024 and 2025 are the clearest measured example.
- **For weed control:** Arkansas research found that low sensitivity settings let pigweed populations grow 280% a year<sup class="cite"><a href="#ref-41" title="Source 41">41</a></sup>.
- **For repair:** Our read: the July 2026 FTC settlement will test whether software-heavy machines can be fixed outside the dealer network.
- **For autonomy:** Deere's Aaron Wetzel said in January 2026 that "we still need some folks in the cab"<sup class="cite"><a href="#ref-24" title="Source 24">24</a></sup>. Our read: the self-driving farm is still a work in progress.

### Talking points

1. Plain GPS is good to about 16 feet; a tractor with RTK corrections can be accurate to about an inch by counting the satellite's radio waves<sup class="cite"><a href="#ref-26" title="Source 26">26</a>,<a href="#ref-17" title="Source 17">17</a>,<a href="#ref-28" title="Source 28">28</a></sup>.
2. A fixed base station with a known position works out how wrong the satellites are and broadcasts the fix to every machine nearby<sup class="cite"><a href="#ref-27" title="Source 27">27</a></sup>.
3. Without guidance, Iowa State measured about 3% overlap in planting and 7% in tillage; RTK guidance can hold overlap to 3-4 inches<sup class="cite"><a href="#ref-34" title="Source 34">34</a>,<a href="#ref-33" title="Source 33">33</a></sup>.
4. See & Spray photographs every plant on a 120-foot boom and decides in milliseconds whether to spray it<sup class="cite"><a href="#ref-20" title="Source 20">20</a>,<a href="#ref-36" title="Source 36">36</a></sup>.
5. Spraying only weeds cut non-residual herbicide use by nearly half in 2025, per Deere, but turning the sensitivity down lets weeds escape and multiply<sup class="cite"><a href="#ref-39" title="Source 39">39</a>,<a href="#ref-40" title="Source 40">40</a>,<a href="#ref-41" title="Source 41">41</a></sup>.
6. Explain it to a friend: Deere sells tractors that steer themselves and sprayers that see weeds, and while its big-farm sales fell 15% last quarter, it forecasts them up 5-15% next year<sup class="cite"><a href="#ref-47" title="Source 47">47</a></sup>.
